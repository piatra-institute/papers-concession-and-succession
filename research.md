# Research

Findings, tiered by source proximity. T1 primary · T2 authoritative secondary · T3 reference · T4 general web (leads only). A claim that reaches the paper rests on a T1 or T2 source. Where only the abstract was obtained, that is stated, and the manuscript asserts no more than the abstract establishes.

## The baseline the paper starts from

- [T1] Nonviolent campaigns have outperformed armed ones at achieving maximalist objectives, on an original dataset of major campaigns from 1900 to 2006; the proposed mechanism is that nonviolent campaigns win legitimacy, attract broader participation, and prompt loyalty shifts and defections among the opponent's security forces, while armed campaigns mobilize comparatively few people and supply a justification for violent counterattack — [@stephan2008], abstract read in full — this is the empirical baseline the model must not contradict, and the reason escalation is treated as generally counterproductive for removal.
- [T1] Contemporaneous armed struggle does not improve the outcomes of unarmed campaigns; there is evidence of an indirect negative effect, since armed struggle is negatively associated with popular participation and thereby with reduced chances of success — [@chenoweth2015], abstract read in full — supplies the participation channel by which escalation costs the campaign the very capacity that produces removal.
- [T1] Security-force defection heavily influences the outcome of nonviolent uprisings; troops receiving economic or political benefits from the regime are more likely to remain loyal, and even unprivileged troops are unlikely to defect if they believe the state can withstand the uprising — [@nepstad2013], abstract read in full — this is why the model makes the incumbent's cost of repression a function of the apparatus's continued cooperation rather than of the campaign's size.
- [T2] Targeting the regime's pillars of support outperforms rapid mass recruitment at generating defections, even for small campaigns, in an agent-based model — [@chenoweth2022dynamic], abstract read in full — an independent formal treatment of the same withdrawal-of-cooperation mechanism, arrived at by a different method.

## Why campaigns escalate anyway

- [T1] Movements are not unitary actors but collections of factions with different preferences over goals and tactics; escalation is likely when a movement contains violence-wielding factions, and particularly likely when the campaign fails to make progress through nonviolent channels — [@ryckman2019], abstract read in full — this is the paper's central conditional. Escalation is selected precisely where the nonviolent route is blocked, which is the corner the model finds.
- [T2] Fragmentation and the organizational structure of a movement shape whether it can sustain a nonviolent strategy — [@pearlman2011], cited for its central argument; the monograph was not read at page level.
- [T1] Repression can be counterproductive: those informed of the regime's use of repression supported renewed protest, and a regime that jails and beats dissidents creates incentives to oppose it long afterward — [@lawrence2016], abstract read in full — the backfire channel the model deliberately does not include, and therefore a stated limitation.
- [T1] Whether repression of unarmed protest backfires depends on organizational infrastructure: a pre-existing campaign infrastructure raises the likelihood of increased domestic mobilization and security defections after violent repression, and parallel media institutions raise both domestic mobilization and international repercussions — [@sutton2014], abstract read in full — this is the strongest external support for treating civilian organizational capacity, rather than grievance or numbers, as the quantity that repression destroys and that escalation spends.

## Why removal is not the outcome that matters

- [T1] Of the 280 autocratic regimes in existence between 1946 and 2010, transitions from one autocracy to another make up about half of all regime changes — [@geddes2014], abstract read in full — the direct warrant for scoring removal, durability and accountability as three separate outcomes rather than one.
- [T1] New democracies growing out of mass mobilization are more likely to survive than those born amid quiescence; the longer the mobilization the more likely survival, across 112 young democracies in 80 countries; sustained *unarmed* uprisings produce the longest-lasting new democracies, largely because they must develop an organizational structure that supplies a leadership cadre, forges links between government and society, and strengthens checks on the post-transition government — [@kadivar2018], abstract read in full — the accountability side of the divergence, and the reason the model's accountability term is a function of surviving *civilian* capacity specifically.
- [T1] Violent revolutions are less likely than nonviolent ones to be reversed, because they produce regimes with strong and loyal armies; violence lowers the likelihood of counterrevolutionary *success* but not of counterrevolutionary *emergence* — [@clarke2022], abstract read in full — the durability side. Read together with Kadivar this is not a contradiction: one is about surviving counterrevolution, the other about accountability to the society that mobilized. The model represents them as two different functions of the same composition variable, which is what produces the three-way divergence.
- [T1] Repression increases the likelihood of a dictator's survival; political terror reduces the likelihood of nonviolent and regular exits, while restrictions on civil liberties deter both organized threats and collective action — [@escriba2013], abstract read in full — repression is not a sign of weakness in the model, and this is why.

## The incumbent's side

- [T2] The canonical formal treatment of the choice between concession and repression under the threat of revolution — [@acemoglu2006], cited for its central argument; the monograph was not read at page level.
- [T1] Ruling parties can concede democratization from a position of strength rather than weakness, when they possess antecedent advantages, suffer setbacks signalling they have passed their apex, and pursue new legitimation strategies — [@slater2013], abstract read in full — a case the present model does not cover, since its incumbent concedes only when conceding is cheapest, and therefore a stated scope limit.
- [T2] Authoritarian rule rests on bargains with subordinates who can withdraw cooperation, and on the problem of power-sharing and control — [@svolik2012]; and rulers survive by paying a winning coalition — [@buenodemesquita2003]. Both cited for their central arguments; neither monograph was read at page level.
- [T1] The less punitive the consequences of losing office, the more a leader is willing to make concessions to strike a peaceful bargain — [@debs2010], abstract read in full — this is the exit-fear term: escalation raises the anticipated cost of leaving power and so raises the price of concession.
- [T1] Dissent and repression are endogenous, and where a government uses preventive repression, observed dissent does not meaningfully predict responsive repression — [@ritter2016], abstract read in full — a caution against reading the model's decision map as an estimate of any observed pattern. Note that this article has a 2024 corrigendum in the same journal; no numeric result from it is used here.
- [T2] Preference falsification means the apparatus's apparent cooperation can collapse without warning, so the observed level of loyalty is a poor guide to its stability — [@kuran1991], cited for its central argument.

## Flank effects, in both directions

- [T1] The presence of a radical flank increases support for a moderate faction in the same movement, in two experiments with 2,772 participants; it is radical *tactics* rather than a radical agenda that drives the effect, and the mechanism is contrast — [@simpson2022], abstract read in full — the strongest case against the paper's conclusion, and it is answered on its own terms: the experiments measure third-party support for a faction, not the campaign's organizational capacity or the character of a successor regime.
- [T3] The origin of the positive radical-flank hypothesis — [@haines1984]. Crossref returns no abstract and the full text was not obtained; cited for the hypothesis's provenance only.
- [T2] Insurgent organizations recruit differently depending on the resources available to them, which shapes what they become — [@weinstein2006], cited for its central argument; the monograph was not read at page level.

## What the search did not settle

- No source was found that models removal, durability and accountability jointly as functions of a single escalation decision. Kadivar and Clarke each hold one outcome fixed and vary the campaign; Chenoweth and Schock hold the outcome at success and vary the flank. The gap this paper fills is the coupling, and the paper claims the coupling as a property of a stipulated model, not as an empirical finding.
- No source was found that gives an empirical estimate of the differential sensitivity of clandestine versus open organizations to repression. The model therefore stipulates the two sensitivities, reports the null in which they are equal, and states that the size of the effect is a function of a parameter nobody has measured.
